# Observation of Gravitational Waves from a Binary Black Hole Merger

## problem
- Albert Einstein predicted the existence of gravitational waves in 1916 from general relativity, but despite indirect evidence (e.g., orbital decay of the binary pulsar PSR B1913+16), a direct detection of gravitational waves had never been made.
- Numerous black hole candidates had been identified through electromagnetic observations, but a binary black hole merger had never been directly observed, and observations in the strong-field, high-velocity regime of gravity were needed to further test general relativity.

## method
- The Advanced LIGO detectors at Hanford, WA and Livingston, LA are modified Michelson interferometers with two 4 km orthogonal arms; each arm has a resonant optical cavity that multiplies the phase effect of a gravitational wave by 300, with power recycling (20 W input built up to 100 kW circulating in each arm cavity) and signal recycling to optimize sensitivity, plus quadruple-pendulum suspension and active seismic isolation of the low-loss fused-silica test masses.
- The coincident signal was first identified by low-latency searches for generic gravitational-wave transients, reported within three minutes of data acquisition, and then confirmed by independent matched-filter analyses using relativistic models of compact binary waveforms.
- Source parameters (masses, spin, distance, redshift) were estimated by fitting general-relativity-based waveform models (some including spin precession) to the data via coherent Bayesian analysis to derive posterior distributions.
- The consistency of the observed signal with general relativity was tested three separate ways: comparing the final black hole's mass/spin inferred from the early versus late stages of the coalescence, checking for deviations in the post-Newtonian waveform phase coefficients, and constraining a modified dispersion relation to bound the graviton's mass.

## dataset
- Real gravitational-wave strain data from the coincident event GW150914, recorded simultaneously by the LIGO Hanford (H1) and LIGO Livingston (L1) detectors on September 14, 2015 at 09:50:45 UTC.
- 16 days of coincident background data, used via a time-shift technique to estimate the event's statistical significance and false alarm rate, plus separate calibration data (from a modulated calibration laser) and several hours of steady-state operation data around the event used to confirm the detectors were behaving nominally; only the two LIGO detectors were observing at the time (Virgo was being upgraded, GEO 600 was operating but not in observational mode).

## results
- The signal swept upward in frequency from 35 to 250 Hz with a peak gravitational-wave strain of 1.0 × 10⁻²¹, matching the waveform predicted by general relativity for the inspiral, merger, and ringdown of a binary black hole system, with a matched-filter signal-to-noise ratio of 24 and a false alarm rate of less than 1 event per 203,000 years (significance greater than 5.1σ).
- The source lies at a luminosity distance of 410 (+160/−180) Mpc, redshift z = 0.09; in the source frame the initial black hole masses are about 36 (+5/−4) and 29 (+4/−4) solar masses, merging into a final black hole of about 62 (+4/−4) solar masses with spin 0.67 (+0.05/−0.07), radiating about 3.0 (+0.5/−0.5) solar masses worth of energy as gravitational waves at a peak luminosity of 3.6 × 10^56 erg/s.
- All three independent consistency checks (final-state mass/spin agreement, post-Newtonian waveform-phase deviations, and a modified dispersion relation) found no evidence of deviation from general relativity in the strong-field regime; the dispersion-relation test bounds the graviton's Compton wavelength to be greater than 10^13 km (graviton mass less than 1.2 × 10⁻²² eV/c²).
- The observation demonstrates the existence of stellar-mass black holes larger than about 25 solar masses and that binary black holes can form and merge within a Hubble time, with inferred local merger rates ranging from 2–400 Gpc⁻³ yr⁻¹ across different population models.

## limitations
- With only the two LIGO detectors observing (Virgo was offline for upgrades and GEO 600 was not in observational mode), the source's sky position could only be localized to an area of about 600 square degrees at 90% credibility, rather than pinpointed precisely.
- The spin of the primary black hole is only loosely constrained (less than 0.7 at 90% credibility) and the spin of the secondary black hole is only weakly constrained by the data.
- The graviton mass bound derived from this single event does not improve on existing model-dependent bounds from galaxy cluster dynamics and weak lensing observations, only on Solar System and binary pulsar bounds.
- This report covers only the single event GW150914; full analysis of the entire first Advanced LIGO observing period was left to future publications, and further sensitivity improvements and additional detectors (Virgo, KAGRA, a possible third LIGO detector in India) were still needed to improve localization and detection rate.

## follow_up_questions

Answerable from the paper:
- Q: What was the matched-filter signal-to-noise ratio of the GW150914 detection?
  A: 24.
- Q: What are the estimated source-frame masses of the two merging black holes and the resulting final black hole?
  A: About 36 and 29 solar masses merging into a final black hole of about 62 solar masses.
- Q: How much energy was radiated as gravitational waves during the merger?
  A: About 3.0 solar masses worth of energy (3.0 M☉c²).
- Q: What was the estimated false alarm rate of the detection?
  A: Less than 1 event per 203,000 years, corresponding to a significance greater than 5.1σ.
- Q: How long are the arms of each Advanced LIGO detector?
  A: 4 km.
- Q: Did the Virgo or KAGRA detectors observe GW150914?
  A: No — only the two LIGO detectors were observing; Virgo was being upgraded at the time, and GEO 600 was operating but not in observational mode.
